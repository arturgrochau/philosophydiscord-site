A Philosophy Book Club on Discord That Reads the Originals

Our book club picks one work of political philosophy or history, sets a reading window, and reads it together. When the window closes we meet on voice and talk the book through, with a text thread carrying the conversation the rest of the time. That's the whole format. It has carried a rotating cast of readers from the ancients to the moderns inside Politics & Philosophy, a Discord community of 1,100+ members with roughly 90 to 100 people online at a time.

The club runs on a conviction the whole server shares: politics is downstream of philosophy and history. If you want to understand a border dispute or an ideology, summaries only take you so far. At some point you have to read what Hobbes actually wrote, or work out what Mill actually meant by harm. Doing that alone is hard. Doing it together turns out to be fun.

How the book club works

Most philosophy book clubs on Discord die of ambition. Someone picks a 700-page tome, four people finish it, the channel goes dead. Ours stays small. One book at a time. The pick rotates, announced in the book club channel with a reading window long enough that people with jobs and exams keep up without heroics.

While the window runs, the channel holds the running thread: passages people loved, passages people want thrown out of the canon, questions that surfaced at 2 a.m. Then comes the voice discussion. Voice is where this server lives anyway (channels run most nights), and talking a book through in real time is a different animal from trading paragraphs in text. You hear people change their minds mid-sentence.

Two practical details worth knowing. There's a book-ping role: opt in and you get notified when a pick is announced or a discussion is scheduled, and the channel stays quiet for you otherwise. And you can join mid-book. Skim the thread, then sit in on the discussion before you've finished. Nobody checks homework here. Ranks on the server are tenure-gated too, earned by showing up over time, and the club is one of the pleasanter reasons to keep showing up.

How we discuss what we read

A book discussion can fail in two directions: it becomes a lecture, or it becomes a shouting match. We steer for neither. The model is Socratic questioning, the method the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on Socrates describes in detail: someone states a position, others test it with questions, and everyone leaves knowing more precisely what they think and why. The aim is to understand a position before you judge it. We wrote up how that works day to day on the civil discussion page; the short version is one non-negotiable rule, arguments over insults, applied to a membership that spans the full political spectrum by design.

A political philosophy reading list to start with

Where should I start is the question we hear most, so here is the club's honest answer: ten entry points, ordered by era. Every one links to a free, public domain edition on Project Gutenberg, downloadable as EPUB, Kindle, or plain text. Don't agonize over translations. Starting matters more.

Ancients

  1. Plato, The Republic: the founding argument over who should rule and whether the just life is worth living; most arguments about justice you'll see online are rehearsals of its opening book.
  2. Aristotle, Politics: Aristotle studied real constitutions before generalizing, and his account of how regimes slide into their corrupted forms still maps onto modern states uncomfortably well.

Early moderns

  1. Machiavelli, The Prince: the first modern book about power, written as working advice with the moralizing left out; every realist take on politics you've heard descends from it.
  2. Hobbes, Leviathan: composed amid the English civil war, it makes the strongest case ever assembled that order comes before liberty; chapters 13 through 21 carry the famous argument if the prose wears you down.
  3. Locke, Second Treatise of Government: consent, property, and the right to resist a government that breaks its trust; the American founding reads like a set of footnotes to it.

Moderns

These five wrote in the shadow of revolutions, and the disagreements between them still define the modern political spectrum. For a map of the tradition Locke opened, the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on liberalism is the reference we hand people.

  1. Rousseau, The Social Contract: "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains" opens the book, and the tension it names between popular sovereignty and personal freedom has never been resolved; the free Cole edition bundles the Discourses too.
  2. Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France: the founding text of modern conservatism, arguing a society is an inheritance you tend, not a machine you rebuild from a blueprint (the free edition sits inside volume 3 of Burke's collected Works, complete).
  3. Mill, On Liberty: the harm principle plus the best case ever made for hearing out opinions you despise; our moderation floor is roughly this book with a report button.
  4. Tocqueville, Democracy in America, Volume 1: a French aristocrat's field notes on democratic habits, tyranny of the majority included, that read like they were filed last week.
  5. Marx and Engels, The Communist Manifesto: short enough to finish in an evening, and half the political vocabulary of the last two centuries stays locked without it, whatever you make of the program.

Where the reading goes next

A book club that never leaves its channel turns into a seminar. Ours leaks, on purpose. Something a member read in Tocqueville can resurface two days later on our world events desk when an election result lands. Expect Hobbes whenever state capacity comes up, and Burke on the Europe desk when constitutional reform is in the news anywhere between Lisbon and Helsinki. That's what the reading is for: a longer memory to think with when the news gets loud.

Some of it even drifts into anicca-chat, our strangest channel, where every message dissolves after 12 hours. The name is the Buddhist term for impermanence. It's a fitting place to float an idea you're not ready to defend yet; we recommend trying it once.

Off Discord, the club keeps a shelf on Goodreads. If that's where you track your reading, join the Politics & Philosophy Book Club group and you'll find the same starter canon there, one note on why each book earned its place.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a philosophy background to join the book club?

No. The reading list above is where most of us started, and the voice discussions only assume you read the book. Asking why Hobbes believes something is often how the best conversations start.

Can I join in the middle of a book?

Yes, and people do. Skim the running text thread to see what's been covered, catch up at whatever pace suits you, and sit in on the next voice discussion even if you're behind. Grab the book-ping role so the next pick reaches you from the start.

Do I have to buy the books?

Rarely. The club leans on public domain texts, and every title on our political philosophy reading list links to a free Project Gutenberg edition in EPUB, Kindle, and plain text. If a pick is still under copyright, libraries and cheap paperbacks cover it.

How do the voice discussions work?

We schedule one when a reading window closes and notify the book-ping role. Someone opens with a question or a passage and the conversation builds from there, Socratic style. The text thread keeps running for anyone who can't make it, and since voice channels run most nights, the book talk tends to spill beyond the discussion itself.
