A European Politics Discord for Europe's Oldest Arguments

Politics & Philosophy is a European politics Discord built on one premise: you can't understand the arguments Europe is having now without the arguments it has already had. We're 1,100+ members, European-leaning but spread across many countries, with roughly 90 to 100 people online at a time. Most of the real conversation happens in voice, most nights. The text channels catch what spills over.

We read, and that shapes everything else. Most online argument about the EU is slogan trading, and it gets tiresome fast. When someone here brings up sovereignty or migration or whether Brussels has too much power, the discussion tends to reach back for what Hobbes or Tocqueville actually wrote, because we treat politics as downstream of philosophy and history. Old books read against current headlines, argued out loud: that's the whole formula.

The arguments are older than the EU

Take the standing fight between Brussels and the national capitals. Strip away the acronyms and it's a question about sovereignty: can it be pooled and divided, or does dividing it destroy it? Hobbes argued in Leviathan that divided sovereignty is no sovereignty at all, which is roughly the case every Eurosceptic makes against the Commission. The integrationist answer leans on Locke's Second Treatise instead: government is a trust held on conditions, and a people may lodge that trust wherever it serves them, including in shared institutions. Neither man ever heard of the EU. Both would recognize the argument inside five minutes.

Migration and citizenship run the same way. Rousseau's Social Contract asks who counts as "the people" whose will makes law legitimate, and every naturalization policy in Europe answers that question whether it admits it or not. On tradition and modernity, Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (Gutenberg has it tucked inside volume three of his collected Works) gave conservatism its founding claim, that institutions carry knowledge no single reformer possesses. Mill's On Liberty is the standing reply:

The despotism of custom is everywhere the standing hindrance to human advancement.

That tension, Burke against Mill, sits underneath half the culture arguments on the continent today. And the first volume of Democracy in America, written by a Frenchman watching a federation from the outside, worries about administrative centralization in ways that read uncomfortably well in 2026 Brussels.

A Europe news desk with locals in the room

The server runs a dedicated Europe news desk alongside the wider world-events desk. Elections, coalition talks, EU summits, national movements, court rulings, migration policy: if it moves in Europe, it lands there. Because membership spans many European countries, a thread on a national election can include someone who votes in it, which beats a foreign correspondent's summary every time. There's also an economics and tech channel for the stories that shape European politics without ever making the front page: energy prices, regulation fights, industrial policy.

When the story is bigger than the continent (great-power competition, alliances, the pressure on Europe's borders from outside), our geopolitics discussions take the wider lens. The two desks feed each other constantly.

Voice nights on European time

Voice is where a European politics Discord earns its keep, and ours runs most nights. Most of us sit in or near European time zones, so the evenings tend to line up: finish dinner in Lisbon or Warsaw and there's a fair chance you'll walk into a live conversation. Some nights it's the day's news from the desk. Other nights one stubborn question, say whether the EU is a federation in denial, can keep a channel going late. Whatever doesn't get settled in voice carries into text afterward, often with book recommendations attached.

Discussion, not point-scoring

One rule is non-negotiable: good faith. The free-expression floor is wide, wide enough that the full political spectrum is present by design, federalists and nationalists in the same voice channel. But arguments aim at positions, never at people. No spam, no raids. Insults are where moderation steps in; disagreement is why the server exists.

The method we try to hold ourselves to is Socratic. Ask what someone actually means. Find out why they believe it. Then test the position and let it stand or fall on its own, which is a different activity from winning. The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on Socrates describes the questioning practice we're borrowing, and our civil discussion page explains how we keep that standard across 1,100 people who agree on very little.

The books underneath it

A rotating book club reads political philosophy and history, ancient to modern, with a dedicated channel and a book-ping role so you only hear about it once you've opted in. The point isn't homework. It's that discussion gets sharper when several people have read the same argument, and the European canon is nearly all free: Gutenberg alone carries Hobbes, Rousseau, Burke, Tocqueville, and Mill in editions that cost nothing. How the club runs, and how the picks rotate, is on the book club page.

Two odd corners

Ranks here are tenure-gated. You climb by showing up over months; message count moves nothing, so there's no incentive to flood chat for status. And there's a channel called anicca-chat, named for the Buddhist term for impermanence, where every message dissolves after 12 hours. It's the spot for thoughts that don't need a permanent record. Half the appeal of the server lives in details like that.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to be European to join?

No. The membership leans European and spans many countries, but outsiders are welcome. If you follow EU politics from the outside, you'll find members happy to explain how a policy lands where they live. Curiosity and good faith are the only real requirements.

Is the server left-wing or right-wing?

Neither, by design. The full political spectrum is present, from convinced federalists to convinced nationalists, and the moderation floor protects free expression. The one hard rule is good faith: no spam, no raids, and arguments aimed at positions rather than people.

When do the voice chats happen?

Voice channels run most nights. Since much of the membership lives in or near European time zones, the busiest hours tend to fall in European evenings. Conversation carries into the text channels afterward, so if you miss a night you can pick up the thread the next day.

Do I have to read the books to take part?

No. The book club is opt-in through a book-ping role, and most channels never assume you've done the reading. That said, the texts we lean on most, from Plato through Mill, are free on Project Gutenberg, so the barrier is low if you get curious.
